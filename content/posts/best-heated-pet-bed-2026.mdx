## Types of Heated Pet Beds

**Electric heated beds** plug into the wall and use a heating element to maintain a set temperature. They warm the bed regardless of ambient temperature. Require a cord (which some pets chew).

**Self-warming beds** use reflective insulation (typically Mylar) to reflect the pet's own body heat back to them. They don't use electricity, so no cords. They only work if the pet is already in the bed and won't warm an empty bed before your pet lies down.

**Microwavable beds** are heated separately and cool over 6–8 hours. Good for short-term warmth; impractical for overnight use.

## Heated Pet Bed Safety Guide

### Temperature Safety: The Most Important Factor

The skin-contact temperature of a heated pet bed must stay below 108°F to prevent burns. This sounds generous, but consider: a pet sleeping in one position for 8 hours on a surface at 110°F can experience low-temperature burns (similar to a heating pad left under a person all night).

**What to look for:**
- Thermostatically regulated beds that auto-regulate to body temperature (102°F) — K&H and a few others
- Customer reviews that mention the bed "running warm" or "getting hot" — these are warning signs
- Beds with an auto-off feature after 6–8 hours

**Avoid:** Beds with maximum temperatures listed at 115°F+, or any bed where the company doesn't disclose maximum surface temperature.

### Cord Safety for Chewing Pets

Electric bed cords are attractive chew targets, especially for young dogs and cats. Before purchasing, check:

- Is the cord covered in steel or heavy-duty sheathing for at least 12 inches from the product?
- Can the cord be tucked behind furniture or run through cable management?
- Does the product use a low-voltage DC adapter (safer than direct AC cords)?

For confirmed cord-chewers, a self-warming bed is a safer choice than electric.

### When to Use and When Not To

**Use heated beds for:**
- Senior dogs or cats (7+ years) in climates with cold winters
- Arthritic pets, especially after surgery or in recovery
- Hairless or thin-coated breeds (Sphynx cats, Greyhounds, Whippets, Vizslas)
- Cats with kidney disease or hyperthyroidism (often feel cold)
- Puppies and kittens in cold environments

**Avoid or monitor carefully:**
- Dogs or cats with incontinence (urine on electric components is a fire risk)
- Pets who sleep in one position and don't adjust — burns can occur from constant contact
- Pets with decreased sensation (diabetic pets, neurological conditions) who can't signal overheating

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I leave a heated pet bed on all day?**

Yes, for thermostatically regulated beds (like K&H products). These maintain a steady safe temperature and can run continuously. For beds without temperature regulation, use a timer outlet set to turn off after 6–8 hours.

**Are heated pet beds safe for cats?**

Yes, when properly selected. Cats seek warm surfaces naturally. Use a thermostatically regulated model, avoid temperatures above 102°F, and ensure your cat has the option to move off the bed if they want to cool down (don't confine them to the heated area).

**Will my dog chew the cord?**

If your dog has chewed cords before, assume yes. Use a cord cover, route behind furniture, or choose a self-warming bed. Never leave a cord-chewing pet unsupervised with an electric bed.

**What size should I get?**

The bed should be large enough for your pet to lie completely flat (not just curled). Measure your pet from nose to tail base and add 6–8 inches. For arthritic dogs that stretch out for relief, go one size up from what the sizing chart suggests.

**Do cats prefer heated beds?**

Most cats strongly prefer warm surfaces — this is why they find sunny spots and computer monitors. Cats with kidney disease, hyperthyroidism, or old age often seek warmth compulsively. A heated bed provides a consistent warm spot and often reduces stress-related hiding.
