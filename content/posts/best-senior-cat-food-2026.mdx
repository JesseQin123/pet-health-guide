# Best Senior Cat Food 2026: Royal Canin vs Hill's vs Purina Pro Plan

At around 7-10 years old, most cats enter their senior years — and their nutritional needs change significantly. Senior cats need:

- **More protein** to preserve muscle mass (muscle loss is the #1 age-related concern in cats)
- **Lower phosphorus** to support kidney function (kidney disease affects ~30% of cats over 15)
- **Joint-supporting nutrients** like glucosamine and omega-3s
- **Highly digestible formulas** because stomach acid production decreases with age
- **Adequate moisture** to prevent urinary issues

Feeding a standard adult cat food to your 11-year-old isn't harmful, but a well-designed senior formula addresses the specific vulnerabilities that come with age.

Here's what the experts — and thousands of senior cat owners — recommend.

## Senior Cat Food Buying Guide

### When Does a Cat Become "Senior"?

Most vets consider cats senior at 7 years, geriatric at 11+, and super-senior at 15+. Larger breeds age a bit slower; smaller breeds may remain active well into their teens.

The AAFCO (Association of American Feed Control Officials) doesn't regulate the term "senior" for pet food — any brand can use it. So look at the actual nutritional profile, not just the label.

### Dry vs Wet Food for Senior Cats

**Wet food advantages for seniors:**
- Higher moisture content (75% vs. 10%) supports kidney and urinary health
- Often more palatable for cats who've lost sense of smell
- Easier to eat for cats with dental issues
- Lower in carbohydrates

**Dry food advantages:**
- More convenient, less expensive per calorie
- Some cats prefer texture
- Stays fresh in bowl longer

Many vets recommend a mix — wet in the morning, dry available throughout the day — especially for senior cats prone to dehydration or kidney issues.

### What Ingredients to Look For

**Good signs:**
- Named protein source (chicken, salmon, turkey) as the first ingredient
- Whole foods and identifiable ingredients
- Omega-3 EPA and DHA (fish oil or direct ingredient)
- Taurine listed (essential amino acid cats can't produce themselves)
- Glucosamine for joint support (especially 10+ years)

**Avoid or limit:**
- Excessive fillers (corn syrup, excessive white rice)
- High phosphorus when kidney issues are present
- Artificial colors, flavors, BHA/BHT preservatives
- Carrageenan in wet foods (linked to inflammation in some studies)

### Phosphorus and Kidney Disease

This is critical. Kidney disease (CKD) affects an estimated 30-40% of cats over 15. Once diagnosed, a low-phosphorus diet is medically necessary — look for foods under 0.5-0.6% phosphorus.

For prevention (not yet diagnosed), senior foods with "moderate" or "restricted" phosphorus are a good proactive choice. Royal Canin Aging 12+ and Purina Pro Plan Senior 11+ both have reduced phosphorus profiles.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Can I feed my 8-year-old cat regular adult food?**
Regular adult food won't hurt healthy 7-8 year old cats, but a senior formula provides age-specific nutrients like glucosamine, higher protein density, and adjusted phosphorus that adult food doesn't prioritize.

**Q: My senior cat is losing weight — what should I do?**
Weight loss in senior cats is always worth a vet check. Common causes include hyperthyroidism, dental pain, kidney disease, diabetes, and cancer — all treatable if caught early. Your vet may recommend a therapeutic diet.

**Q: Should I feed my senior cat more protein?**
Yes — muscle loss (sarcopenia) is the biggest age-related issue for senior cats. Protein helps maintain muscle mass. Look for 35-45% protein in dry food on an "as-fed" basis.

**Q: How much should a senior cat eat?**
Most senior cats eat 20-30% fewer calories than when younger. Follow the feeding guide on your chosen food, adjusted for your cat's actual weight and activity level. Overweight senior cats have more joint, heart, and kidney strain.
